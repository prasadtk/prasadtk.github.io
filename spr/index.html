Physical Principles & Optical Geometry Foundations

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) is an optical, label-free method that measures real-time refractive-index changes within approximately 150–300 nm of a metallic sensor surface.

Because refractive index scales with mass concentration in aqueous buffer, SPR directly monitors macromolecular association and dissociation without fluorescent, enzymatic, or radioactive labels.

Surface plasmon polaritons

At a noble-metal/dielectric interface—typically a 45–50 nm gold film adjacent to aqueous buffer—coherent oscillations of delocalized conduction electrons form surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs).

These are excited using p-polarized monochromatic light, commonly at 650–780 nm.

Kretschmann ATR geometry

Free-space photons lack sufficient momentum to excite SPPs at a planar metal surface. Commercial instruments therefore use the Kretschmann attenuated total reflection configuration.

Light passes through a high-refractive-index prism, strikes a gold-coated chip above the critical angle, and generates an evanescent field that penetrates the thin metal film into the sample layer.

Resonance condition

kx = (2π / λ) · nprism · sin(θSPR)
= (2π / λ) · √[(εmetal · εsample) / (εmetal + εsample)] Equation 1.1 — Momentum-matching resonance condition

The in-plane wavevector of incident light must match the surface-plasmon dispersion relation. Here, ε represents the complex dielectric constants of the metal and sample layer.

Evanescent field decay

The field is strongest at the gold–solution interface and decays exponentially into solution. The characteristic penetration depth, where amplitude falls to 1/e, is typically 100–300 nm.

SPR is therefore sensitive chiefly to events within the immobilized hydrogel matrix and relatively insensitive to distant bulk-solution components.

Resonance units

  • 1 RU = approximately 0.0001°, or 0.1 millidegrees.
  • Globular proteins: 1 RU ≈ 1 pg/mm² surface-bound protein.
  • Protein dn/dc: approximately 0.18–0.19 mL/g.
  • Nucleic acids: dn/dc ≈ 0.17 mL/g, producing roughly 0.8–0.9 RU per pg/mm².
  • Glycoproteins, lipids, carbohydrates: often show lower RU-per-mass ratios.

Sensor Chip Surface Chemistry & Matrices Surface design

Bare gold can adsorb and denature proteins. Modern chips use self-assembled monolayers and hydrogel matrices—commonly carboxymethylated dextran—to preserve activity and provide attachment chemistry.

Direct coupling versus affinity capture: Direct amine coupling is durable and useful for many regeneration cycles, but random lysine coupling can create heterogeneous orientation. Affinity capture generally provides more uniform orientation and is preferred for high-precision kinetic measurements.

Assay Design & Immobilization Experimental design

Use the lowest amount of active, homogeneously oriented ligand that still gives an acceptable signal-to-noise ratio.

Valency rule Immobilize the multivalent partner and inject the monovalent partner. Injecting bivalent analyte over immobilized ligand can create avidity artifacts and violate 1:1 kinetics.
Molecular-weight rule Immobilize the smaller partner and inject the larger partner to amplify response. For molecules below 500 Da, immobilize 3,000–10,000 RU of target.
Stability rule Immobilize the partner that best tolerates low-pH or high-salt regeneration without losing conformational activity.

Theoretical Rmax

Rmax = (MWanalyte / MWligand) × Rligand × n Equation 3.1 — Theoretical maximum binding response

For clean 1:1 protein–protein kinetics, target an experimental Rmax of 30–100 RU. Excess ligand density promotes steric hindrance, rebinding, and mass-transport limitation.

EDC/NHS amine coupling

  1. pH scouting: Test ligand at 10–50 µg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate at pH 4.0–5.5. Avoid Tris, glycine, ammonium ions, and amine-containing stabilizers.
  2. Activation: Inject freshly mixed 1:1 EDC/NHS—0.4 M EDC and 0.1 M NHS—for 7 minutes at 10 µL/min.
  3. Ligand injection: Inject ligand until the desired immobilization level is reached.
  4. Blocking: Inject 1.0 M ethanolamine-HCl, pH 8.5, for 7 minutes.
Acidic ligands: Proteins with pI below 4.5 may not pre-concentrate on carboxymethyl dextran. Use thiol coupling, aldehyde coupling for glycoproteins, or biotin–streptavidin capture instead of lowering pH below 3.5.

Binding Kinetics & Rate Constants Core analysis

SPR resolves association and dissociation in real time, enabling estimation of kinetic constants rather than relying only on endpoint measurements.

1:1 Langmuir model

A + B ⇌ AB
  • ka / kon: Association rate constant, M⁻¹s⁻¹.
  • kd / koff: Dissociation rate constant, s⁻¹.
  • KD: Equilibrium dissociation constant, the concentration at which 50% of sites are occupied.
dR/dt = ka · C · (Rmax − R) − kd · R

KD = kd / ka

t1/2 = ln(2) / kd ≈ 0.693 / kd Equation 4.1 — 1:1 Langmuir binding under continuous flow

Concentration-series design

  • Use at least 5–6 serial dilutions.
  • Span approximately 0.1× to 10× KD.
  • Use 2-fold or 3-fold dilutions.
  • Include duplicate concentrations and blank-buffer injections.

Dissociation monitoring

Allow dissociation to proceed until at least a 5–10% signal decrease is observed. Very tight binders with kd below 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ may require 15–60 minutes of monitoring.

Steady-state affinity

When binding is very rapid, sensorgrams reach equilibrium plateaus and kinetic constants cannot be resolved reliably. Determine KD from equilibrium response versus concentration.

Req = (C · Rmax) / (C + KD) + Offset Equation 4.2 — Steady-state binding isotherm

Complex Binding Models & Mechanisms Model selection

A 1:1 Langmuir model is the default benchmark, but biological systems may require mechanism-aware models. Do not select a complex model solely because it lowers χ².

Mechanistic signatures

Model Scheme Origin Diagnostic signature
Two-state A + B ⇌ AB ⇌ AB* Initial complex undergoes slow structural isomerization. Apparent dissociation slows with longer contact time.
Heterogeneous ligand A + B₁ ⇌ AB₁
A + B₂ ⇌ AB₂		 Independent site populations, often from random coupling. Biphasic curves independent of injection duration.
Bivalent analyte A + B ⇌ AB
AB + B ⇌ AB₂		 Analyte has two binding sites, such as intact IgG. Apparent kd decreases at higher ligand density.
Mass transport Abulk ⇌ Asurface
Asurface + B ⇌ AB		 Surface binding outpaces diffusion through the boundary layer. Initial association becomes linear; rates vary with flow rate.
Parsimony: More parameters often improve fit statistics without improving mechanism. Validate models by varying contact time, ligand density, and flow rate.

Thermodynamics via van ’t Hoff Analysis Temperature series

Measure KD across 4–6 temperatures, commonly 10–37°C, to estimate interaction enthalpy and entropy.

ΔG° = R · T · ln(KD) = ΔH° − T · ΔS°

ln(KD) = (ΔH° / R) · (1 / T) − (ΔS° / R) Equation 6.1 — Gibbs energy and van ’t Hoff relationship
  • Linear plot: ln(KD) versus 1/T gives slope ΔH°/R and intercept −ΔS°/R when ΔCp is negligible.
  • Curved plot: Indicates significant heat-capacity change, often from hydrophobic surface burial and water reorganization.
  • Transition state: Temperature-dependent ka and kd can be analyzed using the Eyring equation.

Small-Molecule & Fragment Screening Low-MW analytes

High-sensitivity SPR can measure 100–500 Da compounds binding immobilized 30–150 kDa targets.

Signal challenge

A 250 Da fragment binding 1:1 to a 50 kDa protein produces only 0.5% of the immobilized-ligand response. Immobilize approximately 4,000–10,000 RU of target to target an Rmax of 20–50 RU.

DMSO correction

Use 1–5% v/v DMSO for solubility. DMSO creates a large bulk refractive-index shift, approximately 1,200 RU per 1% DMSO, while immobilized protein creates an excluded-volume mismatch.

Inject 6–8 DMSO standards spanning ±0.5% around the nominal buffer concentration before and after compound injections.

Data Processing & Double Referencing Mandatory workflow

Raw sensorgrams contain baseline drift, refractive-index jumps, and injection transients. Apply double referencing before kinetic fitting.

1. Align baselines and injection times Zero each sensorgram to the pre-injection baseline, typically 10–20 seconds before t = 0, and align the exact injection-start time across cycles and flow cells.
2. Reference-surface subtraction Subtract the reference flow cell from the active ligand flow cell to remove bulk refractive-index shifts and nonspecific matrix binding.
3. Blank-buffer subtraction Subtract the average of matched running-buffer blanks from reference-subtracted analyte curves to remove systematic flow-cell artifacts and drift.
4. DMSO correction When DMSO is present, apply the solvent-correction curve before blank subtraction to correct excluded-volume effects.

Experimental Artifacts & Troubleshooting Diagnostics

Recognizing physical artifacts is essential for rigorous biophysical interpretation.

Common artifacts

Artifact Root cause Corrective action
Mass-transport limitation High ligand density or fast association depletes analyte near the surface. Test 10, 30, and 100 µL/min. Reduce ligand density below 50 RU and use 30–50 µL/min or higher.
Electrostatic nonspecific binding Positively charged analyte binds negatively charged dextran. Increase NaCl to 150–300 mM, add 0.005–0.05% Tween-20/P20, use soluble dextran competitor, or use CM4.
Carryover Incomplete regeneration causes baseline climb and falling Rmax. Scout glycine-HCl pH 1.5–3.0, NaOH, MgCl₂, or SDS using short pulses; verify repeatability over 10 cycles.
Low experimental Rmax Ligand inactivation, steric occlusion, or wrong stoichiometry. Use oriented capture or reduce immobilization pH/contact time.
Square-wave jumps Sample and running buffer differ in salt, glycerol, or DMSO. Dialyze or desalt analyte into the exact running buffer.

Publication Checklist & QC Standards Reporting

Verify and report these items for reproducible SPR datasets.

Instrument model, sensor-chip chemistry, and experimental temperature.
Complete running-buffer composition, including pH, salt, surfactant, cations, and DMSO.
Coupling chemistry, flow rate, ligand concentration, final RU, and reference preparation.
Association time, dissociation time, flow rate, regeneration solution, and pulse duration.
At least 5–6 concentrations spanning 0.1× to 10× KD, with duplicates and blanks.
Double-referenced sensorgrams, global fits, residuals, ka, kd, KD, Rmax, and errors.
At least 2–3 independent experimental replicates.

Single-Cycle Kinetics Fragile targets

Single-cycle kinetics injects increasing analyte concentrations sequentially without intermediate regeneration.

  • Cumulative dissociation: Fitting must account for residual binding from preceding injections.
  • Time efficiency: Can reduce assay time by up to 70%.
  • Surface preservation: Reduces exposure to harsh regeneration conditions.
  • Best use: Unstable proteins, scarce samples, and high-throughput screening.

Advanced Optical Configurations & LSPR Extensions

Localized SPR

LSPR uses noble-metal nanoparticles rather than continuous films. Its evanescent field decays over roughly 5–10 nm, making it sensitive to binding immediately at the nanoparticle surface.

It supports fiber-optic probes and compact colorimetric biosensing platforms.

MP-SPR and grating coupling

MP-SPR uses multi-wavelength or angular spectral scans to estimate both layer thickness and refractive index, useful for conformational changes, hydrogel swelling, and lipid bilayers.

Grating-coupled SPR replaces the prism with a surface diffraction grating, enabling compact chip-based arrays.
